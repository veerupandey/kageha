# Evidence records

Use stable claim/source identifiers when a matrix or repeated revision needs
them. One row links one precise claim to one source. Reuse these records across
reports, decks, and spreadsheets rather than starting another search by default.

## Record what was established

Useful fields are claim ID/text, source ID/title, URL or local page/section,
publication date, retrieval date, stance, relevant finding, access coverage,
underlying evidence lineage, and limitations. Record study design, population,
sample size, effect/uncertainty, and funding or corrections when relevant and
actually reported. Leave absent metadata unknown; do not invent it to fill cells.

Distinguish a complete source read, a specific section, an abstract, and an
unretrieved lead. A retrieved abstract does not verify details only available in
full text. Store a locator for the evidence used, not merely a homepage URL.
Quotes must fit quotation limits; usually a faithful paraphrase plus locator
serves better than reproducing paragraphs.

Use stance labels such as support, challenge, qualification, mixed, or unresolved.
Keep a source's own conclusion distinct from your interpretation. Different
populations, time horizons, or measurement methods may qualify a claim rather
than directly contradict it. Do not infer causality from a correlation alone.

## Challenge the apparent answer

Search for a plausible alternative explanation, negative/null result, correction,
or newer evidence for a consequential claim. In a fixed source set, inspect that
set and state its limits instead of expanding it without authorization. A lack
of opposing results in a bounded search is not proof that none exist.

Treat syndicated reporting, a press release and its coverage, reanalysis, and
overlapping study cohorts according to their shared evidence lineage. Independent
teams reusing the same data are not independent samples. Judge evidence by its
fit and method rather than a universal source-count threshold. Explain why
stronger evidence outweighs a larger number of weaker sources when relevant.

## Finish the argument

Trace each important conclusion and claimed research gap to the records behind
it. Separate absence of evidence, inaccessible evidence, and evidence against a
claim. Describe a gap as a limitation of the reviewed evidence unless the search
supports a stronger statement. A general synthesis does not establish systematic
review completeness.

For a reproducible review, keep queries, dates, sources searched, scope decisions,
and retrieval failures. For a quick comparison, keep this compact in the working
context. Deliver a file/matrix only when requested or needed by the deliverable;
do not impose fixed report sections, consensus scores, or an arbitrary source
quota on every research task.
